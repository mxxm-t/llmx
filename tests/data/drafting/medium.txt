I am preparing a short guide for new staff at a city bike-share service. Below are the notes our operations team keeps about how the system moves bicycles around the city during a working day. Using these notes, explain how the rebalancing of the bike-share system works, in steps, from the overnight planning to the end of the evening. Write it as clear explanatory prose for someone who has never worked in operations, with one short paragraph for each step, and say why each step matters.

Operations notes:

The network has 412 docking stations and about 5,600 bicycles, of which roughly 1,900 are electric. Each station reports, once a minute, how many bicycles are docked, how many docks are free, which docks are out of order, and the charge level of every electric bicycle that is docked. These reports go to the planning service, which keeps a history of every station going back three years.

Demand is very uneven. On a weekday morning, people ride from the residential districts on the hills down to the offices near the river and the main railway station. By nine o'clock the stations in the centre are full and riders arriving there cannot dock, while the stations on the hills are empty and nobody there can start a trip. In the evening the flow reverses, though it is weaker, because many people take the tram home rather than ride uphill. At weekends the pattern changes again: the parks, the waterfront and the market fill up around midday and empty in the late afternoon.

Every night at two o'clock the planning service builds a forecast for the next day. For each station it estimates, hour by hour, how many bicycles will be taken and how many will be returned. The forecast uses the same weekday from previous weeks, the weather forecast, the school calendar, and any events the city has announced, such as concerts, football matches or road closures. Rain lowers demand sharply, and a warm sunny weekend can double it near the water.

From the forecast the service computes a target for each station: the number of bicycles it should hold at the start of each period so that it neither runs empty nor fills up before the next visit. A station in a residential district gets a high morning target and a low evening target. A station near the offices gets the opposite. The targets also keep a minimum number of free docks at every station, because a rider who cannot return a bicycle is more annoyed than a rider who cannot find one.

The difference between what a station holds and its target is its imbalance. A positive imbalance means bicycles must be taken away, a negative one means bicycles must be brought. The service ranks the stations by how soon each one will run empty or full if nothing is done, not by the size of the imbalance alone.

The fleet of service vehicles has eleven vans that each carry up to twenty-four bicycles, four cargo bikes that each carry up to six, and two night trucks that carry forty. The vans work in two shifts, from five in the morning to one in the afternoon and from one to nine in the evening. The cargo bikes work in the centre, where vans are slow and parking is difficult. The night trucks move large numbers of bicycles between depots and the busiest stations before the morning peak.

Each vehicle gets a route, a list of stations with the number of bicycles to load or unload at each one. The routing tries to pair stations that have too many bicycles with nearby stations that have too few, so that a van rarely drives empty. It also respects the vehicle's capacity, the drivers' breaks, and the time windows in which a station must be served. A route is recomputed every fifteen minutes, because riders keep changing the picture while the vans are on the road. A driver sees the next three stops on a tablet and confirms each load and unload, which updates the station counts immediately.

Electric bicycles add a second task. A docked electric bicycle charges only at the forty stations that have charging docks. At the others, staff swap its battery by hand. The service flags any electric bicycle below twenty percent charge, and the vans carry charged batteries as well as bicycles. A bicycle below five percent is hidden from the app so that nobody starts a trip on it.

Broken bicycles are reported by riders in the app or detected when a dock reports a fault. A bicycle with a reported fault is locked in its dock, and the next van that passes takes it to the workshop. The workshop returns repaired bicycles to the depot, from where the night trucks bring them back into the network.

Riders also help. When a station is about to fill up, the app offers a small credit to riders who return their bicycle to a nearby station with space, and when a station on a hill is empty, it offers a credit for trips that end there. These offers move about a tenth of the bicycles that need moving on a normal weekday, at a much lower cost than a van.

At the end of the evening shift the service compares what happened with the forecast. Stations that ran empty or full for more than ten minutes are listed, with the reason where one is known, such as a van delayed by traffic or a forecast that missed an event. These reports, and the full day of station data, feed the next night's forecast.
